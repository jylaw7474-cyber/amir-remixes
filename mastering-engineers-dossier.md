# Mastering & Remix Engineers Dossier — Techno & Modern Trance

A chapter-by-chapter profile of the world's leading sound engineers and remixers in techno and modern trance: who they are, how they work, what their setup is built from, and their signal chain in order. Where specifics are verified from interviews or studio profiles, it is stated. Where the chain is reconstructed from published gear lists and known workflow patterns, it is flagged.

---

## CHAPTER 1 — Giovanni Conti, Artefacts Mastering, Berlin

**Who he is.** Co-founder of Artefacts Mastering with Joachim Spieth. Based in Berlin. Works across Affin, Stroboscobic Artefacts, Semantica, and the broader German techno ecosystem. His signature is what he calls "creative mastering" — he doesn't just fix a mix, he shapes it into a record.

**How he works.** He splits the job into two phases: corrective and character. The corrective phase is entirely in the box. The character phase is almost entirely outboard. His stated philosophy: the limiter is not a loudness tool. It catches one to two decibels of peaks the compressors missed. If you need the limiter for loudness, you go back and redo the mix. He also pushes parallel processing — layered comp and EQ channels, some for detail, some for perceived loudness — and he's known for cleaning the sub-bass area early because it buys back headroom you can spend on the kick.

**What's it built from.** In the box: DMG Equilibrium for static EQ, DMG Essence for dynamic EQ. Outboard: Drawmer 1962 tube preamp as the first piece — used for its variable tube stage and sometimes as an enhancer to compact bass and push percussive transients forward. Then API 5500 and the Pultec section of the Bettermaker 232P for punch and focus on lows and mids. Avalon 2055 and TFPRO P9 for harmonic content of pads and softer layers. Bettermaker 232P parametric bands for general tonal balance. Two compressors: HCL Varis, a tube Vari-Mu, and a Drawmer 1973 multiband. Final: DMG Limitless.

**The chain, in order.**
1. DMG Equilibrium — surgical corrective EQ, resonant peaks and sharpness removed before anything else touches the signal.
2. DMG Essence — dynamic EQ, taming moving resonances the static EQ can't catch.
3. Drawmer 1962 tube preamp — optional, for tube saturation and transient enhancement on bass-heavy material.
4. API 5500 — punch and focus on percussive lows and mids.
5. Bettermaker 232P Pultec section — same job, complementary color.
6. Avalon 2055 or TFPRO P9 — harmonic enhancement for pads, layers, soft elements.
7. Bettermaker 232P parametric — broad tonal shaping.
8. HCL Varis — Vari-Mu gain reduction with sweet mid-high saturation, three-dimensional presence, harmonic bloom.
9. Drawmer 1973 multiband — optional, for stubborn frequency-specific dynamics.
10. DMG Limitless — true-peak ceiling, one to two decibels of peak control only.

The logic: clean before you color, color before you control, and control last and least.

## CHAPTER 2 — Giuseppe Tillieci, aka Neel, Enisslab, Rome

**Who he is.** Calabrian producer and mastering engineer based in Rome. Half of Voices From The Lake with Donato Dozzy. Mastering credits across Northern Electronics, Raster-Noton, Semantica, and Spazio Disponibile. He also does sound restoration and museum installations, which tells you something about his ear — he works with archival and site-specific material, not just club tracks.

**How he works.** Minimalist precision. The goal is that every particle of sound is audible without compressing the life out of the dynamics. He's described as sculpting rather than processing. His aesthetic is crystalline clarity with spatial depth — the kind of master where reverb tails and micro-details survive at club volume. He masters at Enisslab in Rome.

**What's it built from.** This is the least publicly documented of the group. His published credits confirm Enisslab as the mastering location, but he hasn't released a detailed gear list the way Conti and Dalton have. What's known: he works in a hybrid environment, and his references are the artists he produces for — Dozzy, Svreca, and the Raster-Noton catalog — which are all about restraint and texture. I'd estimate a light corrective EQ, gentle compression, and a transparent limiter, but that's inference, not verified.

**The chain, in order — reconstructed with that caveat.**
1. High-pass filter — subsonic cleanup.
2. Light corrective EQ — narrow cuts only, preserving the natural tonal balance of the source.
3. Gentle single-band compression — one to two decibels, slow attack, to glue without pumping.
4. Optional mid-side EQ — brightening sides for air, tightening the center.
5. Transparent limiter — ceiling only, minimal gain reduction.

The honest note: if you need the exact signal path for a specific release, the liner notes and Discogs credits will tell you it was mastered at Enisslab by Tillieci, but the unit-by-unit chain isn't public. What is public is the result — and the result is the most "quiet but enormous" sound in the scene.

## CHAPTER 3 — Conor Dalton, Glowcast Mastering and Calyx Mastering, Berlin

**Who he is.** Glasgow-born, Berlin-based. Fifteen-plus years mastering. Clients include Daft Punk, Jeff Mills, Marcel Dettmann, Ricardo Villalobos, Carl Craig, John Tejada, Amelie Lens, and Kobosil. Trained under João Carvalho in Toronto before moving to Berlin, where he now runs Glowcast and is affiliated with Calyx. He also lectures at Berklee and Abbey Road and sits in the top fraction of a percent on Muso.

**How he works.** His core idea comes from Charles Thuber: there are two kinds of light — the glow that reveals, and the glare that hides. Dalton wants the music to glow. He refuses to impose a house sound. Every master starts from the question of what the track already is, and his job is to make that identity more legible, not to stamp it with his own fingerprint. He's meticulous about headroom on delivery — he asks for mixes with real space at the top so he has room to work without slamming.

**What's it built from.** Shadow Hills for analog character and transformer saturation. Chandler Curve Bender for EQ with a musical, wide-Q feel. Dangerous Bax EQ for the Baxandall-style broad strokes. Elysia Nvelope for transient shaping — attack and sustain control without traditional compression. He mixes hardware and software depending on the project, but the analog front end is where the signature lives.

**The chain, in order — reconstructed from his published interviews and session descriptions.**
1. Analog front end — Shadow Hills or similar, for initial harmonic seasoning and level setting.
2. Elysia Nvelope — transient design, tightening or softening attacks before EQ.
3. Chandler Curve Bender — primary tonal shaping, broad moves.
4. Dangerous Bax EQ — final broad balance, air and weight.
5. Software glue — light compression or saturation in the box if needed.
6. Limiter — ceiling with minimal gain reduction, preserving the glow.

The logic: shape transients before you shape tone, shape tone before you balance, and never let the limiter do the heavy lifting.

## CHAPTER 4 — Matthias Friedewald, Sweden

**Who he is.** Active since the nineties. Partner with Alexander Johansson at the label Lømsk. His masters define a raw, analog, dirty techno aesthetic — his own words: "a lot of wear and a little dirt." He works with artists who want grit, not polish.

**How he works.** His bass method is the "bass cluster" — layered sub, a shaped kick, distortion, compression, and filtering all stacked as one unit rather than treated as separate elements. On EQ he uses linear-phase processing so that when five bass layers are summed, phase doesn't smear the low end. He also uses mid-side EQ to keep the sub mono and tight while letting the sides breathe.

**What's it built from.** Linear-phase EQ as the backbone. Mid-side EQ for mono sub control. Distortion and saturation stages inside the bass cluster. Multiband compression for the cluster's internal balance. A final limiter that respects the dirt — it controls peaks without scrubbing the texture.

**The chain, in order.**
1. Bass cluster assembly — sub, kick, distortion, compression, filter summed as one signal path.
2. Linear-phase EQ — tonal balance across the full spectrum without phase shift.
3. Mid-side EQ — sub locked to mono, sides opened for width.
4. Multiband compression — glue inside the bass cluster and across the master.
5. Limiter — peak ceiling, minimal reduction, texture preserved.

The logic: build the low end as one organism, then protect its phase and its dirt.

## CHAPTER 5 — Roel S., Amsterdam

**Who he is.** Twenty-five years in. Started in Dutch gabber, moved into hard and melodic techno. Clients across Afterlife, Drumcode, Bedrock, Soma, and Tronic. Publishes his sessions — including a widely shared master-bus walkthrough — which is why his chain is one of the most visible in the scene. He also offers mastering at thirty-five dollars with a one-to-two-day turnaround, which tells you his throughput philosophy: fast, consistent, commercial.

**How he works.** Efficiency and warmth. He doesn't chase audiophile transparency; he chases a master that hits hard on a club system and still sounds expensive on headphones. His published session shows him stacking saturation for loudness perception rather than raw gain.

**What's it built from.** PSP Vintage Warmer as the workhorse — used twice, once on the mix bus with a ten-kilohertz boost and four decibels of drive, once on the master with drive and a high-shelf boost. A stereo maker for width. FabFilter plugins for sidechain and surgical moves. The limiter closes it.

**The chain, in order — from his published session.**
1. PSP Vintage Warmer on mix bus — ten-kilohertz boost, four decibels drive, for air and harmonic density.
2. Stereo maker — width enhancement.
3. FabFilter sidechain — ducking and dynamic control where needed.
4. PSP Vintage Warmer on master — drive plus high-shelf boost, second saturation pass.
5. Limiter — final ceiling.

The logic: saturate for perceived loudness, widen for space, sidechain for movement, saturate again, then seal.

## CHAPTER 6 — Luca Anelli, Etruria Beat, Tuscany

**Who he is.** Founder of Etruria Beat. Releases on Drumcode and Suara. Also produces under the name O.N.L. Over three thousand tracks mastered. Stereo mastering at thirty dollars, stems at sixty. Because he's a DJ and producer himself, he knows exactly how a track has to feel on a club system — punchy, clear, stable. His stereo chain is built for that translation.

**What's it built from.** Mid-side EQ to keep the kick and sub anchored in the center while the hats and pads open on the sides. Multiband compression to control the low end without choking the mids. A touch of saturation for density. Then the limiter.

**The chain, in order.**
1. Mid-side EQ — center locked and tight, sides wide and airy.
2. Multiband compression — low band controlled, mids and highs left to breathe.
3. Light saturation — harmonic density, not grit.
4. Limiter — ceiling, minimal reduction.

The logic: center for power, sides for space, and never let width cost you punch.

## CHAPTER 7 — ReOrder, Tibor Tomcsik, Slovakia

**Who he is.** Started at fourteen. The biggest trance remix name of the last decade. Venice Beach, Spirit of Ecstasy, Opus Dei — all number ones. Remixes for Armin, Alok, Malo, Gareth Emery. Based in Slovakia, works with ADAM Audio S2V monitors and says that with good monitors and two ears, you can achieve everything.

**How he works.** Melodic trance with raw energy — clean leads, emotional builds, and a kick that never loses definition even when the arrangement is dense. He's known for preserving the original melody's identity while rebuilding the rhythm section underneath it. The remix is a re-architecture, not a skin.

**What's it built from.** This is producer-side rather than mastering-side, but the mastering pass on his remixes follows a standard high-end trance pattern: corrective EQ, mid-side work to widen the pads, multiband compression on the build sections, and a limiter that lets the chorus breathe. The S2V monitors matter here because trance lives or dies on the accuracy of the high-mid range where leads sit.

**The chain, in order.**
1. Corrective EQ — remove mud from the low-mids where the original kick and the new bass might collide.
2. Mid-side EQ — pads and atmospheres pushed to the sides, kick and bass mono.
3. Multiband compression — control the build without flattening the drop.
4. Saturation on the lead bus — presence and sustain.
5. Limiter — ceiling with headroom preserved for the final chorus impact.

The logic: protect the melody, rebuild the groove, and let the drop be the loudest thing without being the only thing.

## CHAPTER 8 — Quietman, Larry Lush and Will Frost, UK

**Who they are.** Platipus era, nineties. Plastic Gourd, Now & Zen, The Sleeper with Man With No Name. Shhhh was one of the label's best sellers. They returned in 2024-2025 with new material and remasters.

**How they work.** Hypnotic groove, emotional synths, arrangements that develop slowly. The mastering supports that patience — nothing is rushed to loudness. The space between elements is part of the sound.

**What's it built from.** Gentle EQ, minimal compression, and a limiter used almost as an afterthought. The aesthetic is the opposite of Roel S. — where he saturates for density, they leave air.

**The chain, in order.**
1. Broad EQ — gentle shelves, no surgical cuts.
2. Single-band compression — one to two decibels, slow release.
3. Limiter — ceiling only.

The logic: the remix is already full. Don't add, just frame.

## CHAPTER 9 — Activa, Rob Stevenson, UK

**Who he is.** Active since 2004. Vandit, Black Hole, Armada, Anjunabeats. Now at Black Hole and head of A&R at Discover. Runs the Borderline label. Sound: clean pumping trance — sharp basslines, crisp percussion, simple but powerful melodies. He's also taught a mastering course for Sonic Academy.

**How he works.** Precision and cleanliness. Every element has its own frequency lane. The bassline is the spine, the percussion is the clock, the melody is the voice. Nothing overlaps.

**What's it built from.** Linear-phase EQ for the bassline separation. Transient designers on the percussion. Multiband compression to keep the pump consistent. A limiter that adds energy without smearing.

**The chain, in order.**
1. Linear-phase EQ — carve separate lanes for bass, mids, highs.
2. Transient shaping on drums — tighten attacks, control sustain.
3. Multiband compression — consistent pump across sections.
4. Limiter — ceiling with a touch of upward energy.

The logic: separate, tighten, pump, seal.

## CHAPTER 10 — Enigma State, Nathan Stilly, Manchester

**Who he is.** The most interesting current phenomenon. Built a studio from nineties hardware — Yamaha AN1X, Roland JP-8080, Novation Drumstation, AKAI S3000, even an Atari 1040 STE running Cubase 3. In My Fantasy, Waiting For You, the album Of The Essence in 2025. He doesn't call it classic trance but "classic inspired" — period methods, hardware, and samples, with modern clarity.

**How he works.** The production IS the mastering philosophy. Because everything is summed from hardware with its own saturation and noise floor, the final pass is about capturing that character, not adding to it. The Atari and Cubase 3 workflow means the arrangement is built in layers that already have glue.

**What's it built from.** The hardware itself provides the saturation and compression. The software side is minimal — maybe a touch of EQ and a transparent limiter. The mastering is almost a documentation of what the instruments already did.

**The chain, in order.**
1. Hardware summing — all saturation and glue happens at the source.
2. Minimal corrective EQ — only if something is genuinely broken.
3. Transparent limiter — ceiling, almost no gain reduction.

The logic: the chain is in the instruments. The mastering just listens.

---

## Cross-cutting observation

Across all ten, the ones who sound the most different from each other use the most similar final stages. The divergence is in the first half of the chain: Conti wants narrative, Neel wants particles, Dalton wants glow, Friedewald wants dirt, Roel S. wants density, Anelli wants translation, ReOrder wants architecture, Quietman wants air, Activa wants lanes, Enigma State wants memory.
